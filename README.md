# AI Adoption as an Investment Theme
### B2B Services · Manufacturing · Industrial Services · Distribution
*Market research for a Canadian private-equity audience*

---

## 1. Purpose

This project examines how AI adoption is changing the economics, competitive dynamics, operating models, and investment characteristics of mid-market businesses in four sectors: B2B services, manufacturing, industrial services, and distribution.

It is not a report about artificial intelligence as a technology. It treats AI as an **investment theme** and asks one governing question:

> **How does AI change the economics and competitive structure of B2B services, manufacturing, industrial-services, and distribution businesses, and what should a private-equity investor do differently because of that information?**

Every finding is tested against a simpler question: *why does this matter to an investor?*

---

## 2. Analytical Chain

All analysis follows the same causal sequence. A finding that cannot be carried at least partway along this chain is treated as context, not as an insight.

```
AI adoption
  → operational change
    → productivity / revenue / cost impact
      → financial impact
        → competitive impact
          → investment implications
```

Where evidence stops partway along the chain, the analysis says so explicitly rather than extrapolating to a financial conclusion.

## 3. Scope

### Sectors

| Sector | Sub-segments in scope | Primary economic focus |
|---|---|---|
| **B2B Services** | Professional services, accounting, consulting, customer support, recruitment, engineering, BPO, other knowledge-intensive services | Automation, revenue per employee, labour productivity, delivery cost, SG&A, margins, scalability |
| **Manufacturing** | Predictive maintenance, computer vision / quality control, robotics, production optimization, demand forecasting, supply-chain optimization, engineering AI, digital twins | Downtime, throughput, defect and scrap rates, labour productivity, inventory, utilization, maintenance cost, EBITDA |
| **Industrial Services** | Field services, equipment services, maintenance, inspection, testing, environmental services, industrial contractors, energy services, equipment rental | Technician utilization, scheduling, routing, asset monitoring, downtime, recurring revenue, service margins, retention |
| **Distribution** | Demand forecasting, inventory optimization, pricing, procurement, customer segmentation, sales forecasting, warehouse optimization, logistics | Inventory turns, working capital, gross margin, sales productivity, retention, order accuracy, EBITDA |

### Geography (in priority order)
1. **Canada** — with particular attention to mid-market companies, Alberta and Western Canada, industrial businesses, energy services, and logistics
2. **United States** — used as a comparator, never as a proxy for Canadian adoption without justification
3. **Global** — only where Canadian or U.S. evidence is unavailable

## 4. Research Standards

### 4.1 Source hierarchy

| Tier | Source type | Examples | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 — Primary** | Government and statistical agencies, regulatory filings, company disclosures, industry associations, academic research | Statistics Canada, ISED, Bank of Canada, U.S. Census Bureau, BLS, SEDAR+/EDGAR filings, annual reports, investor presentations, earnings transcripts | Preferred for all quantitative claims |
| **2 — High-quality research** | Consultancies, multilaterals, universities | McKinsey, Bain, BCG, Deloitte, PwC, KPMG, OECD, IMF, World Bank, WEF | Used with methodology noted (survey sample, geography, company size) |
| **3 — Business media** | Reputable financial press | Globe and Mail, Financial Post, WSJ, FT, Bloomberg, Reuters | M&A, company announcements, investment activity, market events |
| **4 — Vendor material** | Vendor case studies and white papers | — | Illustrative only. Always labelled **vendor-reported**; never treated as independent ROI evidence |

**Excluded:** SEO content sites, unsourced market-size reports, AI promotional sites, unattributed statistics, low-quality blogs.

### 4.2 Evidence labels

Every quantitative or factual claim carries one of the following labels:

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Observed** | Directly measured by a primary source (e.g., official survey result) |
| **Reported** | Stated by a company or organization about itself, not independently verified |
| **Estimated** | Third-party estimate with a stated methodology |
| **Calculated** | Derived by this project from cited inputs; calculation shown |
| **Assumed** | Analytical assumption; stated explicitly and never presented as fact |
| **Forecast** | Forward-looking projection; source and horizon identified |

### 4.3 Non-negotiable rules

- **No invented evidence.** Adoption rates, ROI, market sizes, productivity gains, company examples, transaction values, forecasts, and citations are never fabricated. Where reliable data does not exist, the text states: *"Publicly available evidence is limited."*
- **Date discipline.** Every data point carries its reference year or period. Data from different years is not combined without explaining the difference.
- **Methodology disclosure.** When sources are compared, differences in year, geography, company size, industry definition, methodology, and sample size are identified.
- **No leaps from operations to financials.** An operational claim (e.g., "20% less downtime") is not converted into a financial claim (e.g., "EBITDA uplift") unless clearly labelled as a calculation or inference.
- **"Could" vs. "does."** The text distinguishes *"AI could potentially…"* from *"Evidence shows that…"* throughout.

## 5. Investment Analysis Framework

### 5.1 Six-layer test (applied to every major finding)

| Layer | Question |
|---|---|
| 1. **Adoption** | What AI technologies are businesses actually using, and at what stage (pilot vs. production)? |
| 2. **Operational impact** | Which business process changes, and how? |
| 3. **Economic impact** | Revenue, labour productivity, opex, gross margin, working capital, asset utilization, capex |
| 4. **Financial impact** | EBITDA, EBITDA margin, free cash flow, ROIC, revenue growth |
| 5. **Competitive impact** | Does AI differentiate, lower barriers to entry, strengthen a moat, commoditize a capability, or intensify competition? |
| 6. **Investment implication** | What should an investor investigate, underwrite differently, or avoid? |

Both **opportunity and risk** are assessed. AI adoption is not assumed to make a business more attractive.

### 5.2 Private-equity lens

| Deal stage | Questions |
|---|---|
| **Sourcing** | Does AI create new investable themes, or erode existing ones? |
| **Commercial DD** | How is AI affecting customer demand, pricing, competition, and differentiation? |
| **Operational DD** | Can AI improve labour productivity, scheduling, procurement, inventory, pricing, maintenance, sales, customer service? |
| **Financial DD** | What is the plausible effect on growth, gross margin, EBITDA margin, working capital, capex, FCF? |
| **Management assessment** | Does management understand AI, have usable data, a realistic plan, the right capabilities, and measurable KPIs? |
| **Exit** | Does AI change growth, margins, scalability, strategic attractiveness, or the buyer universe? |

AI is not assumed to expand valuation multiples.

### 5.3 Hype vs. economic reality test

Each major AI application is assessed against ten questions:

1. Is it actually deployed?
2. Is it commercially proven?
3. Is there independent evidence?
4. Is the ROI measurable?
5. How long does implementation take?
6. What does implementation cost?
7. Does it require proprietary data?
8. Can competitors replicate it?
9. Does it affect EBITDA?
10. Is management's claimed benefit supported by evidence?

## 6. Research Process

1. Define the question
2. Identify relevant data
3. Find primary sources first
4. Cross-check important claims
5. Compare methodologies
6. Identify conflicting evidence
7. Separate fact from interpretation
8. Translate findings into investment implications
9. Identify what remains unknown
10. State limitations

The objective is synthesis, not source collection.

---

## 7. Workplan

The project deliberately builds evidence and framework **before** producing a polished report.

| Phase | Objective | Output |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Evidence base** | Establish credible adoption data for Canada and the U.S. by sector, company size, and year | Adoption dataset; source log with methodology notes |
| **2. Application mapping** | Identify which AI applications are deployed in each sector and at what maturity | Application dataset by sector |
| **3. Economic and financial impact** | Collect independent and reported evidence on operational and financial outcomes | Financial-impact dataset with evidence labels |
| **4. Company evidence** | Document real company examples from filings, transcripts, and disclosures | Company case log |
| **5. Competitive analysis** | Assess where AI builds moats vs. commoditizes capabilities, by sector | Sector competitive assessments |
| **6. Investment implications** | Translate findings into sourcing themes, DD questions, value-creation levers, and risks | Investment-implications matrix; DD checklist |
| **7. Gap analysis** | Document where evidence is weak and what additional research is needed | Evidence-gap register |
| **8. Synthesis** | Produce the final report only once phases 1–7 are substantively complete | Final report and presentation |

## 9. Chart Standards

Charts are used only where they improve understanding. Every chart includes:

- A clear title
- Source(s)
- Reference date or period
- Units
- Methodology notes where necessary (sample, definitions, weighting)

Charts are never built from fabricated, estimated-without-disclosure, or unsupported data.

Candidate charts: adoption by industry; adoption over time; adoption by company size; Canada vs. U.S. adoption; application categories by sector; investment and M&A activity; sector exposure.

## 11. Writing Standards

Concise, analytical, skeptical, commercially focused, and data-driven. Written as professional investment research.

**Avoid:** hype, marketing language, generic statements, unsupported predictions, and unnecessary technical or AI-specific jargon.

## 12. Known Limitations (to be updated)

- Canadian AI adoption data at sector and company-size granularity is likely thinner than U.S. data; regional (Alberta / Western Canada) cuts may be limited or unavailable.
- Survey-based adoption measures vary in how they define "AI use," which limits comparability across sources and years.
- Private mid-market companies rarely disclose AI-related financial outcomes; public-company and vendor evidence may skew toward larger firms and positive results.
- Financial impact evidence is expected to be weaker than operational impact evidence, and both weaker than adoption evidence.

These limitations are tracked in the evidence-gap register and addressed as the research progresses.
